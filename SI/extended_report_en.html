1. Why focus on linkage structure instead of citing share alone?

The share of policy documents citing science is a useful starting point. But improving policy evidence practice requires one extra step: seeing where evidence accumulates and how it moves across channels.

Furnas et al. (2025), published in Science in April 2025, shows two signals in U.S. policy documents.
First, the share of documents citing at least one scientific paper rises from around 20% in 1995 to around 35% in 2023.
Second, cross-partisan co-citation rates remain low (often single-digit), although values vary by document type.
In short, “citing more often” and “sharing the same evidence” are different questions.

This shift in question is possible because of Overton.
Overton collects policy documents and extracts references, linking scholarly citations (e.g., DOI-matched works) as well as policy-to-policy citations (Szomszor & Adie, 2022).
Overton is not a complete census of all policy documents; coverage depends on accessible sources. Coverage itself can affect country/type comparisons, so we cross-check it in Methods: scope and Appendix: coverage & tagging.
This analysis is further limited to the DOI-observable policy-to-research scope to diagnose the linkage structure of where evidence accumulates and how it is transmitted.

There is also a complementary SDG-framed reading of Overton.
The Springer Nature SDG Impact Report classifies 2015-2025 Overton policy documents into SDG vs non-SDG groups and reports that the “at least one scholarly citation” share is 11.6% for SDG documents and 3.4% for non-SDG documents.
It also points to asymmetries such as knowledge-brokering roles (IGO/NGO/think tanks), Global North concentration, and low domestic-research citation shares in many countries (Springer Nature, n.d.).

The SDG report centers on a goal framework, while this report centers on linkage structure.
They are complementary, not competing frames.
Also, prior indicators and this report’s indicators should not be directly compared without checking denominators first.3

1.1 Why these questions emerged

These questions were not chosen arbitrarily. They came from three observations.

  • Observation 1: Prior studies describe “how much is cited” well, but explain “where evidence converges” less.
  • Observation 2: In the Overton-OpenAlex linked data, concentration repeatedly appears in top domains and top policy sources.
  • Observation 3: For Korea, inbound and procurement routes show different structural signals when separated.

Therefore, the text checks two questions.

  • Q1: Is policy evidence distributed broadly, or concentrated in hub channels?
  • H1 (working assumption): Policy evidence will show relatively concentrated patterns around a small set of hub channels.
  • Q2: Are Korea’s inbound and procurement evidence routes symmetric?
  • H2 (working assumption): Korea will show asymmetric patterns across domains and countries.

1.2 Time and concentration: baseline pattern of linkage structure

  • Observation: DOI citation volume grows rapidly from the mid-2010s and peaks around 2021.
  • Interpretation: The post-2022 decline should not be read as an immediate reversal before checking lag effects (roughly 4-5 years) between publication and citation accumulation.
  • Observation: Under multi-label domain aggregation, the top three domains account for 88.8%.
  • Interpretation: This is not a better/worse ranking claim; it is a structural observation that policy evidence demand is concentrated in specific agenda/domain channels.
  • Monitoring signal: Track whether the top-3 domain share eases, stays flat, or rises in the next snapshot.

1.3 Topics do not move at the same speed

  • Observation: Unique work counts and policy citation counts do not always move together.
  • Interpretation: High research production in a topic does not automatically imply high policy uptake.
  • Interpretation: “Mature topics” and “high-growth topics” coexist in policy evidence use; update cycles and synthesis speed can differ by agenda.
  • Monitoring signal: Check whether high-recency topics keep their position in the next cycle.

Chapter 1 takeaway
What matters more than a single citing-share number is the linkage structure of where evidence accumulates and how it is transmitted.

2. Where evidence accumulates: hub structure

Chapter 2 directly tests Q1: is policy evidence broadly distributed, or concentrated in a small number of channels?

Chapter 2 key line
When total volume and density are separated, hub concentration becomes clearer.

2.1 Total volume and density send different signals

  • Observation: Figure 5A (total volume) and Figure 5B (per-document density) do not share the same top order.
  • Interpretation: “Channels that produce many documents” and “channels that pack dense evidence per document” should be read separately.
  • Monitoring signal: Track whether overlap between top-volume and top-density groups expands or shrinks.

2.2 Evidence-use patterns vary by source type

  • Observation: The same volume-vs-density split appears by source type.
  • Interpretation: A type with large total volume does not necessarily have high per-document citation density.
  • Interpretation guardrail: Source-type tags are not mutually exclusive; cross-type pattern differences matter more than raw shares.
  • Observation: Domain composition differs clearly by source type.
  • Interpretation: Policy evidence use reflects not only research-side supply, but also source-role and agenda composition.
  • Monitoring signal: Check whether domain skew strengthens within specific source types.

Chapter 2 takeaway
Hub concentration can improve transmission efficiency, but higher concentration can also increase sensitivity to shocks in a few channels. That is why repeated monitoring should track whether top-channel concentration is rising, stable, or easing.

3. Korea’s position: asymmetry between inbound and procurement

Chapter 3 directly tests Q2. It does not reduce Korea to a single score, and separates inbound (who cites Korean research) from procurement (whose research Korean policy cites).

Chapter 3 key line
“Who cites Korean research” and “whose research Korea cites” are not the same structure.

3.1 Korea’s domain demand-supply mix is misaligned

  • Observation: In Figure 6, Korea is below the diagonal in Social Sciences and above it in Health/Physical/Life.
  • Interpretation: Domain-level demand/supply mismatch exists, so inbound strategy and procurement strategy should be separated.
  • Monitoring signal: Track widening/narrowing of absolute domain gaps (pp).

3.2 Where Korean policy procures evidence

  • Observation: Procurement portfolios differ in breadth across policy sources.
  • Interpretation: Multilateral sources (e.g., IGOs) are broader, while some sources show stronger domestic/single-country dependence.
  • Monitoring signal: Track Korea’s top-1 country share and top-3 combined share.

3.3 Korea’s self-citation share and two-way routes

  • Observation: Korea’s self-citation share is about 4.1% on the known-citation denominator.
  • Interpretation: The Korean sample (2,742 policy-source documents) is small and affected by missingness, so this is a trend-check indicator rather than a ranking signal.
  • Interpretation guardrail: Expected-vs-observed is relative to a domain-weighted baseline, not an absolute score.
  • Observation: Inbound is led by the U.K. (32.8%), IGOs (16.3%), and the U.S. (16.2%), while procurement is dominated by U.S. research (57.3%, known-citation basis).
  • Interpretation: Different top channels across directions indicate structural asymmetry.
  • Interpretation guardrail: Inbound/procurement shares are normalized within each direction and should not be read as absolute cross-direction magnitudes.
  • Monitoring signal: Track inbound top-3 concentration and procurement top-1 dependence separately.

Figure 12 summarizes the same asymmetry as a network view.
Because it is a reduced map with top nodes and top links only, it should be read as a large-route structure tool, not as a full-network representation.

Chapter 3 takeaway
Korea’s inbound and procurement routes follow different structures. Strategy should therefore be route-specific rather than a single one-score prescription.

4.2 Follow-up analysis roadmap (short)

  • Include non-DOI evidence (reports, web sources, legal texts)
  • Add policy-to-policy citation layers
  • Separate citation-context functions (evidence use vs background mention vs critique)
  • Connect policy agenda classifications (e.g., SDGs) with topic classifications as a dual frame

5. Interpretation scope and Extended Report guide

Chapter 5 fixes the interpretation boundary for the conclusions above.

5.1 What this brief does say

  • Observation: Policy evidence is repeatedly concentrated in a small set of hub channels (Figures 2, 5, 7, 8).
  • Observation: Korea’s inbound and procurement routes follow different structural patterns (Figures 6, 9, 10, 11, 12).
  • Interpretation: Route-specific repeated monitoring is a higher priority than one-score ranking.

5.2 What this brief does not say

  • Interpretation boundary: Citation volume is not treated as direct policy impact/performance.
  • Scope boundary: Non-DOI evidence (web/legal/report citations) is outside default scope.
  • Time boundary: Korea-specific numbers can vary with sample size, missingness, and snapshot updates.

5.4 Closing note

Two headline findings remain: evidence is concentrated in hub channels, and Korea’s inbound/procurement routes are asymmetric.

To stabilize Korea-focused comparisons, domestic policy references need to be structured with identifiers (DOI/URL) and recomputed in the same frame.
Kim, Park, & Yoon (2025) provides an NKIS-based starting point for that direction.

The next step is to standardize the observation system, not to amplify conclusions.
