A2. Why exclude sub-tags (A > B) and keep top-level country tags only

Overton policy source country tags include country-level tags (e.g., USA, UK) and sub-regional tags (e.g., USA > California). To align meaning with OpenAlex country codes, this report uses top-level country tags only for default aggregations.

This simplifies country-level comparisons. At the same time, it can invite under/over-interpretation when sub-tags contribute heavily. So, when reading country comparisons, it is safer to check coverage itself as well.

A3. Why do we need exceptions for Hong Kong / Georgia: symptom → cause → action

Policy source country tags can appear only as sub-tags such as China > Hong Kong and USA > Georgia. If we keep only top-level tags, these can be filtered out from the policy-source side. That can make them look like “policy sources that do not exist” in the network view. For network figures, we therefore split China > Hong Kong into Hong Kong and USA > Georgia into Georgia (USA).

More detail: sub-tag scale, examples, and Hong Kong notation

Data snapshot: Overton 2025.02

The numbers below are summaries computed from the Overton 2025.02 snapshot.

  • By policy document counts: sub-tags total 200,381 out of 1,671,835 (12.0%)
  • By DOI citation records: sub-tags total 3,488,024 out of 48,456,680 (7.2%)

Here, “DOI citation records” are raw-record totals, and may differ from analysis-ready deduplicated counts shown in the main report key-number block.

Top-level parents with large sub-tag contributions are typically USA, Spain, Canada, Australia, UK (in that order).

Is Hong Kong “merged into China” here?

In the current snapshot, Overton policy sources do not include Hong Kong as a top-level country tag. It is observed only as China > Hong Kong (1,182 policy documents). By contrast, OpenAlex research countries use a separate code HK → Hong Kong.

If we aggregate policy sources using top-level tags only, documents under China > Hong Kong drop from the policy-source side. Meanwhile, Hong Kong remains only as a research-country node. This can make it look like a “cited-only node.”

Practical caution: naive sub-tag replacement causes collisions

If you naively map A > B → B, then USA > Georgia (the US state) collides with Georgia (the country). If you want to re-attribute sub-tags at country scale, it is safer to separate two steps: (1) lifting to parent (A > B → A) and (2) exception handling (whether to treat Hong Kong as its own unit).

B. Definitions & interpretation

This section compiles denominator/definition/guardrail items needed to re-read main-report metrics safely.

  • Observation baseline: the same number can imply different conclusions when denominators differ.
  • Check purpose: confirm metric definitions and missingness conditions before interpretation.

B0. Quick definitions (summary)

  • Unit of analysis: Overton policy documents are deduplicated at the policy-document level and treated as “unique policy documents.” See: DOI-citation coverage share
  • Citation counts vs unique works: “how often” vs “how widely” are different questions, so we separate them. See: Citations vs works
  • Recency / intensity: recency is the share of policy citations to works published in 2022-2024; intensity is average policy citations per unique work. See: Recency / intensity
  • Multi-label topics: OpenAlex topics are multi-label; a single work can belong to multiple topics, so shares can exceed 100%. See: Multi-label
  • Self-citation denominator: self-citation share is computed only over citations with known first-author country codes. See: Self-citation share

B1. DOI-citation coverage share (97.6%): denominator

What denominator does “97.6%” use in this snapshot?

We deduplicated Overton policy documents at the policy-document level to build 1,381,084 unique policy documents. Then we counted whether each document contains at least one DOI-based scholarly citation. As a result, 1,347,563 documents (97.6%) contain at least one DOI citation.

Note

Interpretation boundary

  • This is not the share of “all policy documents” that cite science. It is the share within the set of policy documents collected in the Overton snapshot.
  • This setup does not capture citations without DOIs (narrative citations, reports, legislation, web sources, etc.).

What does “deduplication” mean here?

Raw Overton policy documents can include duplicates (versions, re-posts, language variants, metadata duplication). For analysis, we unify them at the policy-document level to treat them as unique policy documents.

B2. Repeat-citation share (48.1%): definition

What does “48.1% are repeatedly cited (>=2)” mean in this analysis snapshot?

This metric summarizes, among unique cited works, how many are called repeatedly across different policy documents.

  • Unit: unique cited works (OpenAlex works)
  • Computation: after cleaning policy→work DOI links, count how many distinct policy documents (unique documents) cite each work
  • Definition: “repeatedly cited (>=2)” means cited by at least two distinct policy documents

In the Overton 2025.02×OpenAlex 2025.06 linked data, 3,143,234 out of 6,532,365 unique cited works (48.1%) are cited by two or more policy documents.

Note

Interpretation boundary

  • As with the 97.6% statistic, this number is limited to the DOI-linked observed scope in the snapshot.
  • Citations without DOIs (reports, legislation, web sources, etc.) are not included in this repeated-call calculation.

B3. Citations vs works

Why separate “citation counts” and “unique works”?

  • Citation counts: total number of DOI citation records (how many times policy documents cite DOI works)
  • Unique works: the number of distinct cited works after deduplicating citations by work identity

The questions “how often does policy cite evidence?” (frequency) and “how wide is the evidence pool?” (diversity) are different, so we interpret them separately.

B4. Recency / intensity

How are “recency” and “citation intensity” computed?

  • Recency: the share of policy citations to works published in 2022-2024
  • Citation intensity: average policy citations per unique work in the aggregation unit = citations / unique works

B5. Self-citation share

How is self-citation share computed?

We map policy source country tags to 2-letter country codes (e.g., USA → US, UK → GB), and compute self-citation share using only citations with known first-author country codes as the denominator. That is, self-citation share is not a pattern over “all citations.” It is a pattern over citations where the first-author country code is available.

More: expected-share definition and a comparison table

How is expected self-citation share computed?

For each policy source country, we first compute its domain mix of cited research. For each domain, we multiply by the country’s global research production share in that domain (OpenAlex), then sum across domains. Conceptually:

expected = sum( policy_citation_domain_share * global_production_share_in_domain )

The table below summarizes self-citation share for a small comparison set. Since self-citation share is computed only over citations with known first-author country codes, it is better treated as a diagnostic metric rather than a ranking.

Here, “share known” is the fraction of DOI citation records for that policy source where the first-author country code is available. For sources with many sub-regional tags (e.g., USA, UK), we aggregated sub-tags back up to the parent country for this summary.

Why define policy-source → research-country flows by first-author country?

First-author affiliation country is the most consistently extractable country attribute. However, it does not reflect coauthor distributions, corresponding authors, or multi-affiliation cases, so it must be interpreted as an “approximation with limited representativeness.”

B6. Multi-label topics

Why can topic/domain shares exceed 100%?

OpenAlex topics are multi-label. A single work can belong to multiple topics simultaneously, so aggregations by topic/domain/subfield can double-count. Topic-based numbers should be read as “topic-instance based.”

In the topic data used here, a single policy citation is linked to about 2.8 topics on average. Therefore, summing by domain/topic yields totals larger than raw citation counts.

However, if we fractionally allocate each citation across the work’s topic count, the domain ranking is preserved and the top-3 domain share changes only slightly (e.g., 88.8% → 88.5%).

Can source-country / source-type totals also grow beyond 100%?

Yes. Overton source country/type labels are tag-based (including parent/child tags), so a single document can be counted in multiple categories.

B7. Recent-year decline caution

Does the post-2022 decline in the time trend mean “policy cites science less”?

A direct conclusion is difficult here. Coverage delays (collection/processing lags) and the fact that citations accumulate after publication likely act together. It is safer to read recent years as signals mixed with observation lag, rather than as real-time decreases.

B8. Why 2012 looks high

Why does “citations per policy document” look especially high around 2012?

When the distribution has a long tail, averages are strongly affected. When the average looks high, it is safer to first separate whether it is driven by a few extreme documents or whether the whole distribution shifted rightward.

Trimmed means exclude extreme values and help diagnose whether the average is being pulled by outliers.

The figure below shows, by year, the mean along with the median and trimmed mean. It also reports the share of citations contributed by the top 1% of documents.

Key messages:

  • The rise around 2012 appears not only in the mean but also in the median and trimmed mean. This suggests a mixture of distribution-wide change, not only a handful of extreme documents.
  • The top-1% contribution does not spike uniquely in 2012. Therefore, it is hard to attribute the 2012 peak to only a very small number of documents.

B9. OA interpretation

Is higher OA (open access) share in policy citations causal?

Observationally, OA share tends to be higher in policy-cited works. But this does not immediately imply causality (“OA causes more policy citation”). Confounders include the likelihood that policy-relevant work is published OA, public funding structure, and domain composition.

However, even when we split by domain and publication-year windows among policy-cited works, the pattern that OA works have higher average policy citations per paper largely remains. The table below compares 2000-2009 and 2010-2019. Recent years are excluded due to stronger observation-lag effects.
